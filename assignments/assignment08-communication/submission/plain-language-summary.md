This summary is for public-health analysts preparing an introductory briefing on BMI and income. It asks how average body mass index (BMI), measured in kg/m², differs across income groups among adults aged 20–80 in the prepared NHANES classroom dataset.

My Assignment 6 analysis included 50,851 records with BMI, age, and income group recorded. Average BMI was 28.6 in the high-income group, 29.4 in the low-income group, and 29.3 in the middle-income group. These rounded averages differed by less than one BMI unit. The table describes the included records; it does not measure obesity prevalence or explain why the groups differ.

The results are unweighted: they do not account for how NHANES sampled participants to represent the US adult population. Records missing BMI or income group were excluded after missingness was reported. If excluded records differ from those retained, the comparison may be biased. Differences such as age between income groups could also influence the averages.

The useful takeaway is that the included groups have slightly different average BMI values, while the reasons remain uncertain. Before using this pattern to guide a public-health decision, analysts would need to account for the sampling design, examine which records were excluded, and consider differences between the groups. This classroom comparison cannot establish that income causes BMI differences.
